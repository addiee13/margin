Why AI Has to Get Lighter

Large models are capable but expensive to run. Lecture 2 names three levers that make them smaller and faster: fewer bits per weight, fewer weights, and hardware built for both. It then sets up the vocabulary Lecture 3 needs.

Bigger models work, but they cost more to run

Large language models show emergent behaviors such as zero- and few-shot learning and chain-of-thought reasoning. [Lecture2, slide 6] [slide 8] [Wei et al., 2022] The catch is size: the capability "comes at the cost of large model size." [slide 7] Running that model on a laptop, a car, or a robot is hard. Those devices are resource-constrained and low-power, sometimes have no internet, and users do not want personal data uploaded to the cloud. [slide 13]

The capability is cheap to ask for. Memory, speed and power are the bill.

The lecture's worked example makes the trade concrete. LLaMA-2-7B runs at 50 tokens per second in FP16 and at 166 tokens per second with 4-bit weights and AWQ, about 3.3 times faster on a 4090 GPU. [slide 14]

Quantization and pruning shrink the model, and hardware has to follow

Quantization stores each weight in fewer bits. The slides name AWQ, which quantizes an LLM to 4-bit weights [AWQ, 2023] and also works on vision-language models. [slides 14, 18] SmoothQuant is cited for post-training quantization of LLMs. [slide 11] Pruning removes what the model does not need. SpAtten prunes redundant tokens. [slide 12]

The hardware half is on slide 22. Single-chip inference performance rose 317 times in eight years, from the K20X at 3.94 Int8 TOPS to the A100 with structured sparsity at 1,248 TOPS. [slide 22]

The slides close this section with the gap. Edge AI runs on a system-on-module or a microcontroller, which is a single chip with a processor, memory and I/O. [slides 27–28] The lecture's summary is that edge devices "still have huge gap to cloud processors." [slide 29]

The vocabulary Lecture 3 builds on

The slides set out three steps: review neural-network terms, review the building blocks, then introduce the efficiency metrics. [slide 33] The first building block is the fully connected layer, where every output neuron connects to every input. Written as one matrix multiply, it is Y = XWᵀ + b. [slide 36]

yi = Σj wij xj + bi

Each output is a weighted sum of its inputs, plus a bias. The slides use five features of a person, each weighted by how much it matters for a basketball team, as the example. [slide 37] The efficiency metrics the next lecture uses are parameter count, model size, peak activations, MACs, FLOPs, latency, and throughput. [slide 33]

What this does not show

The slides give benchmark numbers for specific models and chips at specific dates. They do not show the accuracy cost of 4-bit quantization or of pruning. The GPU table reproduces the lecture's charts and has not been checked against NVIDIA's datasheets. The 317-times figure on slide 22 is the lecture's own claim. The slides also give no numbers for phones compared with the cloud.
