Which deletion hurts least?

Pruning chooses a smaller set of parameters while controlling prediction error. Learn the mathematics behind magnitude scores, curvature scores, and rebuilding a layer from fewer channels.

Open the course review ↗

Course basis: Dr. Haoxin Wang, GSU, Efficient AI Lectures 5–6; the slides acknowledge selected material from Prof. Song Han’s MIT course on TinyML and Efficient Deep Learning Computing. Numerical examples and derivations below are original teaching extensions, not reported experiments. [Lecture6, slide 1]

1 · “At most N” includes exactly N surviving weights

Start with four weights [3, −2, 1, −5] and a budget of two nonzeros. [3, 0, 0, −5] satisfies that budget; a strict “fewer than two” inequality would reject it. The course review asks both how pruning is formulated and which criterion selects weights. [Lecture6, slide 56]

minWP L(WP)   subject to   ‖WP‖0 ≤ N

WP contains the pruned model’s weights; L is its training objective evaluated on a fixed dataset; N is the permitted nonzero count. arg min returns the weights attaining the minimum, whereas min returns the minimum loss value. The notation ‖·‖0 counts nonzeros. Despite its common name “L0 norm,” it is not a true norm: doubling a nonzero vector leaves this count unchanged, violating a norm’s scaling rule. The constraint restricts support—the positions allowed to be nonzero—while optimization may change their values.

Reading correction: Lecture5 slide 5 prints < N in its main formula but says “at most N” and uses ≤ N in its right-hand diagram. Lecture6 slide 56 uses ≤ N. The existing broad pruning page repeats < N; use ≤ N for an inclusive nonzero budget. [Lecture5, slide 5]

A mask implements WP = M ⊙ W, where each mask entry is 0 or 1 and ⊙ multiplies matching entries. This sets values to zero while preserving array dimensions. Rebuilding a structurally pruned layer removes dimensions and adjusts the adjacent layer. Zeroing a channel is therefore a different implementation state from deleting its stored channel dimension. The course’s reconstruction diagram illustrates the removed input columns and corresponding weight rows. [Lecture6, slide 16]

2 · Magnitude scores are easy to compute but depend on scale

For one weight, the magnitude score is |w|. For a group S of weights, L1 adds absolute values; L2 takes the square root of their squared sum. The course presents these as heuristics, rather than exact loss changes. [Lecture5, slides 29–32]

Original arithmetic, using the slide’s matrix
GroupL1L2
Row [3, −2]3 + 2 = 5√(9 + 4) = √13 ≈ 3.606
Row [1, −5]1 + 5 = 6√(1 + 25) = √26 ≈ 5.099

Removing the lower-scoring row deletes [3, −2]. Removing two individual weights instead deletes −2 and 1. The pruning unit determines what a score ranks. A group containing four unit weights has L1 = 4 and L2 = 2; a group containing one unit weight has both scores 1. Raw scores therefore mix typical weight size with group size. Mean absolute magnitude (L1 divided by group size) or root mean square magnitude (L2 divided by the square root of group size) gives 1 for both examples. These are alternative heuristics, not automatic corrections to the original objective.

Now trace one scalar output: z = 0.1a1 + 1a2. With activations a1 = 100 and a2 = 1, the contributions are 10 and 1. Deleting the smaller weight changes this output more. Rescale the first activation to a′1 = 0.01a1 and its weight to 10: predictions remain identical, but magnitude ranking reverses. This original counterexample shows why a small weight alone does not prove small influence. Cross-layer comparisons need care when activation scales differ.

Scaling factors rank entire output channels

The course multiplies each channel’s output by a trainable scaling factor; call it γ here. The slide lists [1.17, 0.10, 0.29, 0.82, 0.56]. If the budget requires removing one of these five channels, ranking |γ| removes the channel with 0.10, leaving four. An illustrative threshold |γ| < 0.30 instead removes 0.10 and 0.29, leaving three. Threshold selection is an added exercise, not the slide’s specified rule. Multiplying activation 2 by γ = 0.10 gives 0.20; deleting the channel makes it zero. This heuristic still needs comparable activation scales: γ alone is not a measured loss change. [Lecture6, slide 12]

APoZ counts observed zeros rather than small weights

ReLU outputs max(0, input), turning negative inputs into zeros. Average Percentage of Zeros (APoZ) is zero activation entries divided by all observed entries for that channel. In the slide, each channel has two 4 × 4 maps: 32 entries. Zero counts are 5 + 6, 5 + 7, and 6 + 8. Thus APoZ is 11/32 = 34.375%, 12/32 = 37.5%, and 14/32 = 43.75%. Under the heuristic that more zeros suggest less usefulness, remove channel 2 first. [Lecture6, slide 15]

Collect representative calibration inputs. A high APoZ channel may detect an infrequent but important feature. Even 100% zeros on a finite calibration batch does not prove a globally dead ReLU—one that remains inactive across the relevant input distribution. These activation statistics and scaling scores rank candidates; evaluation must establish whether deletion is acceptable.

3 · Loss, gradient, and curvature answer different questions

A loss function turns prediction disagreement into a scalar. For example, a target 3 and prediction 2 have squared error (2 − 3)2 = 1. A training loss usually aggregates errors across examples. We hold that aggregation fixed when comparing the original and pruned model; otherwise numerical loss changes are not comparable.

The gradient g collects first derivatives, gi = ∂L/∂wi: the local slope when one weight changes. The Hessian H collects second derivatives, hij = ∂²L/(∂wi∂wj): how slopes change. A large diagonal hii means strong curvature along coordinate i. An off-diagonal entry measures coupling between coordinates. These are the ingredients of the course’s second-order criterion. [Lecture6, slide 13]

Define the actual perturbation δw = WP − W and the signed loss change ΔL = L(WP) − L(W). Positive ΔL means harm; negative ΔL means improvement. A second-order Taylor approximation gives:

ΔL ≈ gTδw + ½ δwTHδw

Here transpose, written T, turns a column into a row so multiplication produces a scalar. For one weight, this is slope × change plus half curvature × change squared. Near a stationary point, all gradient entries are approximately zero. Stationary does not by itself mean a minimum; nonnegative curvature is needed for a nonnegative quadratic damage estimate.

Sign correction: Lecture6 slide 13 and the existing broad page write original loss minus pruned loss while calling the result an increase. This page consistently uses pruned minus original. With this convention, deleting weight wi means δwi = −wi.

4 · OBD can prefer deleting the larger weight

Optimal Brain Damage (OBD) ranks the estimated damage of deleting a weight. The course drops the first-order term near convergence, drops cross terms under a diagonal Hessian approximation, and assumes a sufficiently accurate quadratic model. With only coordinate i deleted:

si ≈ ½ hii(−wi)² = ½ hiiwi²

In this original example, let w = [0.2, 1], g = 0, and H = diag(100, 1). “diag” puts the listed numbers on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. Smaller saliency s means the cheaper deletion.

CandidateMagnitudeOBD calculationEstimated ΔL
Delete w10.2½ × 100 × 0.2²2
Delete w21½ × 1 × 1²0.5

Magnitude deletes the first weight; OBD deletes the second. The smaller weight lies in a steeper direction. If all diagonal curvatures were the same positive value, these rankings would agree because squaring preserves the ordering of nonnegative magnitudes.

Derive the approximation and inspect its limits

For several deletions, the quadratic term is ½ Σihiiδwi² + Σi<jhijδwiδwj for a symmetric Hessian. Single-coordinate deletion has no cross terms even without a diagonal assumption; multiple deletions generally do. If h12 = h21 = 5 and both example weights are deleted, the cross contribution is 5 × (−0.2) × (−1) = 1. Total damage is 2 + 0.5 + 1 = 3.5, rather than 2.5. If g1 = 15 instead of zero, deleting the first weight gives −15 × 0.2 + 2 = −1 under the quadratic model. Dropping the gradient would miss the predicted improvement. Finally, deletion is a finite step; a local Taylor approximation can fail over that distance. Scores computed before fine-tuning do not automatically describe the refitted model.

5 · Layer reconstruction fits a matrix of target outputs

Use the lecture’s row-per-example convention. With batch size b, input channel count ci, and output count co, X has shape b × ci, W has shape co × ci, and Z = XWT has shape b × co. Each output is a dot product: multiply matching input and weight entries, then add. Biases are omitted in this example. The target Z is the original layer’s output, not the dataset’s class label.

A channel contribution is the outer product XcWcT, where Xc is a b × 1 input column and Wc is a co × 1 weight column. A selector/scaling coefficient βc controls that contribution; zero deletes the channel. [Lecture6, slide 17]

minW,β ‖Z − Σc βcXcWcT‖F²
subject to ‖β‖0 ≤ Nc

The squared Frobenius norm adds the squared entries of the error matrix: ‖E‖F² = Σn,oEno². Errors [[1, −2], [0, 3]] give 1 + 4 + 0 + 9 = 14. Nc is the permitted nonzero channel count. A nonzero β may scale a channel, rather than equal 1; its scale can be absorbed into the surviving weights. Joint scaling of β and W therefore need not identify unique coefficients.

6 · Reweighting a survivor can recover a correlated deleted channel

Take three examples, two inputs, and one output: X = [[1, 2], [2, 4], [3, 6]], W = [[1, 1]]. Matrix multiplication gives target Z = [3, 6, 9]T. Keep only the first input, so β = [1, 0]. The second input was exactly twice the first; its information can be represented by the survivor.

StagePredictionsSquared reconstruction error
Delete channel 2; keep weight 1[1, 2, 3]2² + 4² + 6² = 56
Refit surviving weight to 3[3, 6, 9]0

The fitted scalar v minimizes Σn(zn − vxn)². Setting its derivative to zero gives v = (Σxnzn)/(Σxn²) = (3 + 12 + 27)/(1 + 4 + 9) = 42/14 = 3. This is least squares: choose weights that minimize squared residuals. Keeping the second channel instead permits weight 1.5 and also gives zero error; this example does not establish a unique best channel.

The lecture alternates two subproblems: fix W and solve β to select channels; fix β and solve W to reconstruct outputs. [Lecture6, slide 18] Small examples can enumerate supports; larger problems use approximate selection. Least squares refits a chosen support but does not solve the combinatorial support search by itself. Alternation does not guarantee the global optimum.

After refitting, a physically rebuilt layer has XP of shape 3 × 1 and WP of shape 1 × 1. A masked version still carries two input columns. Correlation also limits generalization: on a new input [1, 0], the original output is 1 while the fitted survivor outputs 3. Perfect reconstruction on the calibration batch does not imply perfect behavior elsewhere.

7 · Choose a criterion by its evidence and limitations

CriterionGainCost or failure
MagnitudeSimple weight-only rankingScale-sensitive; ignores activations and loss geometry
OBDUses local loss curvatureRequires curvature estimates; finite deletions and interactions can defeat approximations
ReconstructionUses calibration inputs and refits survivorsLocal output fidelity may not preserve final task accuracy or unseen behavior

What this does not show

These calculations establish arithmetic and assumptions, not measured accuracy or speed. They do not select all per-layer pruning ratios, prove a hardware speedup, or establish an optimal sparse model. Final task evaluation, appropriate calibration data, and checks of rebuilt dimensions remain necessary. The two source inconsistencies identified above should not be memorized as mathematical identities.

Retrieve the ideas without looking

  1. Can a model keep exactly N weights under an “at most N” budget?
    Answer

    Yes: nonzero count ≤ N. Strict < N excludes the boundary.

  2. What sign does a harmful deletion have, and what perturbation deletes w?
    Answer

    ΔL = L(WP) − L(W) is positive. Deletion gives δw = −w.

  3. Which toy weight does OBD delete?
    Answer

    The weight 1: damage 0.5 versus 2 for weight 0.2. Magnitude makes the opposite choice.

  4. Why does the surviving regression weight become 3?
    Answer

    The deleted input is twice the survivor; both original weights are 1. Thus x + 2x = 3x. Least squares gives 42/14 = 3.

  5. Which scaling factor is smallest, and which channel has the highest APoZ?
    Answer

    Scaling removes the displayed 0.10 factor first. APoZ removes channel 2 first: 14/32 = 43.75%, exceeding 11/32 and 12/32.

  6. Does zero reconstruction error on three examples prove preserved accuracy?
    Answer

    No. Input [1, 0] breaks the observed correlation and gives output 3 instead of 1.
